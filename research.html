Research

I build instruments for astronomy's fastest signals. Fast radio bursts and pulsar pulses change within milliseconds or less, and optical measurements at a matching cadence remain rare. My work spans radio-array electronics, fast optical detectors, and the timing that lets the two observe the same sky at the same moment.

Radio

Radio-array instrumentation

Radio arrays find transients by watching large areas of sky continuously. I work on the analog hardware between the antenna and the digitizer, where noise, interference, cost and manufacturability decide what an array can do.

2023 – present

CHORD

The Canadian Hydrogen Observatory and Radio-transient Detector (CHORD) is a next-generation radio telescope with a planned core of 512 six-metre dishes operating at 300–1500 MHz, plus outrigger stations for very long baseline interferometry. It builds on CHIME to study fast radio bursts, cosmology and fundamental physics.

My role. I lead the team developing the analog signal transport from the low-noise front ends to the digitizers: amplification, filtering and gain equalization, and a remotely monitored power system. We validate these systems on the CHORD pathfinder and oversee manufacturing and integration for the core array. I also contributed to the construction of the CHIME/FRB Outriggers.

Papers

  • CHIME/FRB Outriggers: Design Overview ApJ, 2025

2023 – present

CHARTS

The Canadian–Chilean Array for Radio Transient Studies (CHARTS) is a wide-field, low-frequency interferometer that will monitor the southern sky for bright, nearby fast radio bursts, using 256 broad-beam antennas at 300–500 MHz. Its band and broad beams demand careful control of interference, calibration, noise and cost.

My role. I lead antenna design, low-noise amplification, analog transport, frequency-division multiplexing and front-end digitization, and I supervise students' electronics and FPGA work from schematic through to field validation.

Papers

  • Differential Active Planar Antenna and Frequency-Division Multiplexing with Commercial Electronics for CHARTS Interferometer EuCAP, 2026
  • Active Planar Antenna Design for CHARTS Array ISAP, 2025
  • Canadian-Chilean Array for Radio Transient Studies (CHARTS): Analog System Developments EuCAP, 2025

2023 – present

Differential electronics and radio-over-fibre

Optical fibre is immune to electromagnetic interference and can carry radio signals over long distances in place of coaxial cable. I developed a low-cost, compact analog radio-over-fibre link from commercially available components, and designed a link that is deployed in the signal chain of the 46 m telescope at the Algonquin Radio Observatory.

My role. I am the principal investigator and supervise the students on this work, which also includes a low-cost, ultrawideband differential low-noise amplifier for interferometric radio telescopes.

Papers

  • Developing a low-cost, compact radio-over-fiber (RFoF) linkage for radio telescope arrays Proc. SPIE, 2024
  • Low-cost, ultrawideband, differential low-noise amplifier for interferometric radio telescopes Proc. SPIE, 2026

2026 – present

SimpleClock

Instruments at different sites need a common time reference before their data can be combined. SimpleClock is a low-cost GNSS-disciplined oven-controlled oscillator that supplies 10 MHz and pulse-per-second references, with autonomous firmware and thermal control.

My role. I lead its development and validation. The hardware and firmware are open source.

Optical

Fast optical detectors

A conventional camera exposure is far longer than the events I want to see. These projects push optical detectors to millisecond and nanosecond timescales, and work out how to tell a real flash from detector noise.

2018 – 2023 · Ph.D. thesis

SPINA: a single-photon imager

The Single-Photon Imager for Nanosecond Astrophysics (SPINA) was built for the Ultra-Fast Astronomy programme led by Prof. George F. Smoot, which surveys the optical sky on millisecond and shorter timescales. SPINA uses a position-sensitive silicon photomultiplier and records each photon's arrival time, position and pulse height with about 8 ns timing.

Its readout pairs a 16-channel analog front end originally made for medical ultrasound with an FPGA system-on-chip, which decodes the position of each event in real time and generates the sensor's bias voltages, all on a single board.

My role. I designed, built, characterized and fielded SPINA, including its electronics, cooling and mechanics. Its first on-sky tests were in July 2022 on the Nazarbayev University Transient Telescope at the Assy-Turgen Astrophysical Observatory.

Papers

  • Initial On-Sky Performance Testing of the Single-Photon Imager for Nanosecond Astrophysics (SPINA) System IEEE Trans. Instrum. Meas., 2023
  • A SiPM photon-counting readout system for Ultra-Fast Astronomy Open Journal of Astrophysics, 2022
  • Development of position-sensitive photon-counting imager for Ultra-Fast-Astronomy Proc. SPIE, 2022
  • On-sky silicon photomultiplier detector performance measurements for millisecond to sub-microsecond optical source variability studies JATIS, 2020

Published 2025

How faint a flash can a detector claim?

A signal-to-noise threshold that looks safe can still produce many false detections when a search covers a very large number of short time windows, especially if the detector noise is correlated. I developed a false-alarm-rate framework that ties the detection threshold to the observing duration, the search timescale and the measured detector statistics, so that characterizing an instrument translates directly into the confidence of an astronomical claim.

Papers

  • False alarm rate-based statistical detection limit for astronomical photon detectors JATIS, 2025

2023 – present

sCMOS cameras and LUVCam

Scientific CMOS sensors now reach very low read noise at high frame rates, at a cost that makes arrays of cameras practical. I contributed to characterizing sCMOS detectors for optical astronomy, and I work on the Little UV Camera (LUVCam), a low-cost ultraviolet and optical camera for space. LUVCam1 has flown as a technology demonstration, and LUVCam2 is its successor.

My role. I develop the readout electronics and FPGA firmware for the LUVCam cameras, and supervise student hardware and firmware work.

Papers

  • LUVCam: A high-performance, low-cost, UV/optical camera for the future of astronomy in space submitted to JATIS, 2026
  • The second little ultraviolet camera (LUVCam2) mission Proc. SPIE, 2026
  • Beyond CCDs: characterization of sCMOS detectors for optical astronomy Proc. SPIE, 2024

Radio + optical

Observing both at once

Radio signals are delayed and distorted by the plasma they cross, while optical light arrives almost unaffected. Catching the same event in both gives a clean reference for its timing and energy.

2024 – present

IQUEYE at Gemini South

IQUEYE, the Italian Quantum Eye, is a single-photon instrument that time-tags individual photons. It was installed as a visitor instrument on the 8.1 m Gemini South telescope for a coordinated pulsar campaign with simultaneous radio coverage. We detected optical pulsations from the Crab pulsar, which validates the timing and provides data for comparing radio and optical pulses.

My role. I took part in installing and commissioning the instrument and led the simultaneous radio observations with the 46 m telescope at the Algonquin Radio Observatory. I also applied the photon-statistics framework from SPINA to the sensitivity and false-alarm analysis.

Papers

  • IQUEYE at Gemini South: instrument, science commission, and first results Proc. SPIE, 2025

Where this is going

No prompt optical counterpart to a fast radio burst has yet been confirmed. A prompt flash may be over before a radio alert reaches a follow-up telescope, so the optical data have to be recorded continuously and searched afterwards, using the time and position of the radio burst.

My aim is to pair wide-field radio arrays with fast, low-noise sCMOS cameras that watch the same field, bringing the timing, continuous acquisition and calibration methods of radio arrays to optical cameras. A detection would constrain how these bursts are produced. Even a non-detection rules out models that predict brighter flashes.
